# Playbook: prediction challenges on simulated markets

Use this when the market is generated by a simulator (HRT Arcade challenges, IMC Prosperity-style rounds, Kaggle synthetic-market contests). A simulator is a finite program; the fastest route to a top score is usually to reverse-engineer it, not to fit a black box. Everything here is a starting point to verify on the data, not a fact about a new challenge.

## 1. First-hour forensics checklist

Run these in roughly this order; each takes minutes.

1. **Label definition.** Recompute the target from raw prices: simple or log return, which bars, how it is demeaned or adjusted. Check it is exact (max diff ~1e-15).
2. **Shapes and ids.** Sessions, bars per session, train/test id ranges, any session with no events.
3. **Text or event templates.** Strip numbers and named entities, then count unique templates. A few dozen templates means a categorical variable, not language. Test whether the numbers and slot words inside templates matter (here: they did not).
4. **Event study.** For each event type, average the per-bar return at lags −3…+20 after the event. Read off the response shape (here: constant rate for 10 bars, half weight at lags 0 and 10, nothing outside) and whether anything leaks before the event.
5. **Entity structure.** Co-occurrence of entities across sessions; cluster the co-occurrence lift matrix (here: 5 sectors × 16 companies, 3 + 1 per session). Use names only if clustering agrees with them; the suffix heuristic misplaced several companies.
6. **Arrival process.** Events per session, per bar and per entity; whether early counts predict later counts.
7. **Volatility.** Distribution of per-session volatility; whether event effects scale with it (here: proportional).

## 2. Event-driven generators

- Fit event amplitudes by least squares on **all bars of all training sessions** (about 1M bar observations), not on the 10k labels: far more data.
- The forecast is usually the effect **still owed** at the decision time: amplitude × remaining kernel weight inside the holding window. Only events published within one kernel length before the decision matter.
- Check additivity: realised vs predicted bar drift when several events overlap (here: additive, slope 1.24–1.29 with pooled amplitudes).

## 3. Latent structure

Simulators often hide a variable that decides which events matter: which entity is the asset, which regime is on.
- **Diagnose it:** pairs of events about the same entity in a session should have correlated responses; pairs about different entities show what the exposure structure is (here: +0.80 same company, −0.23 different).
- **Model it:** EM over the hidden variable, using all 100 bars of training sessions; at decision time a posterior from the observed bars only.
- **Calibrate it:** if the posterior needs flattening (temperature > 1) to score best, the likelihood is misspecified (exposure structure or noise model). That is a lead worth more than another feature.

## 4. Noise and volatility

- Per-session volatility from **Garman–Klass range variance** (plus squared opening gaps) beats close-to-close sd by a lot with 50 bars.
- Test for mean reversion: regress future residual returns on the part of the past move that events do not explain (here: negative slope, +0.07 Sharpe as one feature).
- Test whether event effects scale with volatility; if so, work in volatility units.

## 5. Turning forecasts into positions for a Sharpe score

With score = Sharpe of `position × return` across sessions, and conditional mean `mu` and variance `s²` of a session's return, the first-order optimal position is

    position ∝ mu / (s² + mu²)

Large forecasts are damped, and noisy sessions shrink. In practice `s² = k · per-bar variance × horizon` with `k` fitted (a flat optimum). Only relative size across sessions matters, so scale is irrelevant. Measured here: this rule beat `mu/s^p` for every p by about 0.07.

## 6. Headroom analysis

Before spending time on marginal features, measure what each missing piece of information would be worth:
- Replace one estimated quantity with an **oracle** (its value estimated from data the model cannot use, e.g. bars after the decision, or the true value in a simulation) and rerun the full pipeline.
- The ingredient whose oracle gives the biggest jump is the bottleneck. Here, knowing which company is the stock lifted 3.59 → 4.2, while ~30 extra features moved the score by less than 0.01 each. That should have been measured at minute 20, not minute 75.
- Oracles are diagnostics only. Never upload anything that uses them.

## 7. Blending

- Blend at unit standard deviation per component; position-level blends did as well as forecast-level blends.
- Structurally different models blend well even at 0.93–0.95 correlation (+0.03 to +0.07 here). Variants of one model do not.
- Weights near the cross-validated optimum are flat; don't tune them past the second decimal.

## 8. Things that did not work last time

Sentiment, TF-IDF and word features on templated text; the numbers and slot words inside headlines; gradient boosting on the structural features; recent price momentum; company-specific volatility or strength; pair-specific peer effects; fitted variance models beyond range variance; semi-supervised fitting on test-set first halves (+0.001 to +0.006). A new simulator may differ, but these deserve a lower prior.
